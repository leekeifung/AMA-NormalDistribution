🔔 Topic 1: The Normal Distribution $X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ in the Real World
Slides 3–11 Motivation

In Lecture Notes 11 (§11.2.1), we learn that continuous random variables—such as physical measurements, temperatures, and machine outputs—are modeled by a bell-shaped probability density function $f(x)$ symmetric about its mean $\mu$, where the area under $y = f(x)$ over $[a, b]$ gives the probability $P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx$.

Example A1 • Smart Retail ☕

PolyU Campus Automated Espresso Dispenser

An automated coffee machine at a PolyU catering outlet dispenses hot americanos into 250 mL paper cups. Due to slight water-pressure fluctuations, the actual liquid volume $X$ dispensed per cup is a continuous random variable following $X \sim N(\mu = 242\text{ mL},\, \sigma^2 = 4^2\text{ mL}^2)$.

Practical Question: What is the probability that a cup overflows ($X > 250\text{ mL}$), and what if the technician recalibrates the mean $\mu$?

Mathematical Model: $X \sim N(242, 4^2)$
$$P(X > 250) = P\left(Z > \frac{250 - 242}{4}\right) = P(Z > 2.00) = \mathbf{0.0228}$$
Only 2.28% of cups overflow!
Example A2 • Climate & Energy 🌡️

Hong Kong Observatory Mid-November Temperature

Historical meteorological records at the Hong Kong Observatory (Tsim Sha Tsui) show that the daily afternoon maximum temperature $X$ in mid-November follows a normal distribution with mean $\mu = 25.0^\circ\text{C}$ and standard deviation $\sigma = 2.0^\circ\text{C}$, i.e., $X \sim N(25.0, 2.0^2)$.

Practical Question: CLP Power engineers want to know the probability that tomorrow's peak temperature stays in the comfortable range $[22.0^\circ\text{C},\, 28.0^\circ\text{C}]$.

Mathematical Model: $X \sim N(25.0, 2.0^2)$
$$P(22 \le X \le 28) = P(-1.50 \le Z \le 1.50) = 1 - 2(0.0668) = \mathbf{0.8664}$$
86.64% chance of mild weather
Example A3 • Precision Engineering 🔩

High-Speed Railway Bolt Diameter Quality Control

A precision CNC lathe manufactures titanium bolts for railway tracks with target diameter $\mu = 12.00\text{ mm}$ and standard deviation $\sigma = 0.02\text{ mm}$, so $X \sim N(12.00, 0.02^2)$. Any bolt outside $[11.95\text{ mm},\, 12.05\text{ mm}]$ is scrapped as defective.

Practical Question: What proportion of bolts pass quality inspection?

Mathematical Model: $X \sim N(12.00, 0.02^2)$
$$P(11.95 \le X \le 12.05) = P(-2.50 \le Z \le 2.50) = 1 - 2(0.00621) = \mathbf{0.9876}$$
98.76% yield rate
📏 Topic 2: Standardizing a Normal Random Variable ($Z = \frac{X - \mu}{\sigma} \sim N(0, 1)$)
Slides 12–19 Motivation

Why do we standardize $X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ into $Z = \frac{X-\mu}{\sigma} \sim N(0,1)$? First, it allows us to use a single Standard Normal Table for any $(\mu, \sigma)$ in the universe (Theorem 3.1). Second, $Z$-scores measure how many standard deviations an outcome lies above or below the mean—allowing fair comparisons across completely different distributions!

Example A5 • Academic Advising 🎓

Comparing Scores Across Two Exam Papers

As Academic Advisor & DELO, suppose two students apply for a scholarship from different cohorts:
• Alice scored $X_1 = 82$ in a cohort where marks followed $N(70, 10^2)$.
• Bob scored $X_2 = 78$ in a harder cohort where marks followed $N(64, 7^2)$.

Standardization Insight: Who performed better relative to their peers?

• Alice's $Z$-score: $z_1 = \frac{82 - 70}{10} = \mathbf{+1.20}$ (Top $11.51\%$)
• Bob's $Z$-score: $z_2 = \frac{78 - 64}{7} = \mathbf{+2.00}$ (Top $2.28\%$)
Bob ($Z = +2.00$) outperformed 97.72% of his cohort!
Example A6 • Inverse Quantiles (Ex 3.5 & 4.2) 🔋

EV Battery Warranty Period Engineering

An electric vehicle (EV) battery pack's service life before dropping below 80% capacity follows $X \sim N(\mu = 9.5\text{ years},\, \sigma^2 = 1.2^2\text{ years}^2)$. The manufacturer wants to guarantee a free replacement warranty period of $w$ years such that only 1% of batteries fail before $w$ ($P(X \le w) = 0.01$).

Reverse Table Lookup (Slide 17, Ex 3.5):
From $P(Z \le b) = 0.01 \iff P(Z \ge -b) = 0.01$, table gives $b = -2.33$:
$$\frac{w - 9.5}{1.2} = -2.33 \implies w = 9.5 - 2.33(1.2) = \mathbf{6.70\text{ years}}$$
📊 Topic 3: Normal Distribution Approximating Binomial Distribution (with Continuity Correction)
Slides 20–28 & 35–39 Motivation

When $X \sim \text{Binomial}(n, p)$ has large $n$ (such as $n = 100$, $900$, or $10,000$), computing exact sums $\sum \binom{n}{i} p^i (1-p)^{n-i}$ by hand is computationally prohibitive (Slide 20). Whenever $\mathbf{np > 5}$ and $\mathbf{n(1-p) > 5}$ (Theorem 5.1), $X$ is closely approximated by $V \sim N(\mu = np,\, \sigma^2 = np(1-p))$ with a $\mathbf{\pm 0.5}$ Continuity Correction to bridge discrete integers and continuous intervals.

Example A7 • Public Policy Poll (Ex 5.1–5.3) 🗳️

Large-Scale Survey of $n = 10,000$ Residents

Suppose $p = 40\%$ of citizens support a new harbourfront pedestrian boardwalk. A university polling center surveys $n = 10,000$ randomly chosen citizens. Let $X \sim \text{Binomial}(10000, 0.4)$ be the number of supporters.

Why Normal Approximation? Summing 901 terms $\sum_{i=3000}^{3900} \binom{10000}{i} 0.4^i 0.6^{10000-i}$ overflows a pocket calculator, whereas $V \sim N(4000, 2400)$ solves it in two lines!

With Continuity Correction ($[3000, 3900] \to [2999.5, 3900.5]$):
$$P(2999.5 \le V \le 3900.5) = P(-20.42 \le Z \le -2.03) = \mathbf{0.0212}$$
Exact R software sum (Slide 25): 0.02101496!
Example A8 • Aviation Operations ✈️

HKIA Widebody Flight Overbooking Risk

An airline at Hong Kong International Airport sells $n = 400$ tickets for a 370-seat Airbus A350 because historical data shows each ticketed passenger independently shows up with probability $p = 0.90$.

Why $+0.5$ Continuity Correction Matters: The flight is overbooked only if strictly more than 370 passengers show up ($P(X > 370)$). Since $X = 370$ is safe, we exclude bar 370 and start at $\mathbf{370.5}$!

$np = 360 > 5,\; np(1-p) = 36 > 5 \implies V \sim N(360, 6^2)$
$$P(X > 370) \approx P(V \ge 370.5) = P\left(Z \ge \frac{370.5 - 360}{6}\right) = P(Z \ge 1.75) = \mathbf{0.0401}$$
Example A9 • Logistics SLA Audit (Ex 7.2) 📦

Regional Sorting Hub Parcel Mis-Delivery

In automated e-commerce parcel sorting (Slide 37, Ex 7.2), $p = 5\%$ of parcels require manual re-routing due to damaged barcodes. Among $n = 900$ randomly audited parcels, management wants the probability that at least 50 parcels ($X \ge 50$) are mis-delivered.

Why $-0.5$ Continuity Correction Matters: Because $X = 50$ is included in $X \ge 50$, we include the entire bar $[49.5, 50.5]$ by starting at $\mathbf{49.5}$!

$np = 45 > 5,\; np(1-p) = 42.75 > 5 \implies Y \sim N(45, 42.75)$
$$P(X \ge 50) \approx P(Y > 49.5) = P\left(Z > \frac{4.5}{\sqrt{42.75}}\right) = P(Z > 0.69) = \mathbf{0.2451}$$

Slides 8–19 Interactive
Definition 2.1 & Theorem 2.1: Normal Distribution $N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ Slide 9
We say $X$ has a normal distribution $N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with parameters $\sigma > 0$ and $\mu$ if its density function for $-\infty < x < \infty$ is: $$f(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\left\{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right\}$$ Furthermore, $\mathbb{E}[X] = \mu$, $\text{Var}(X) = \sigma^2$, and the curve is symmetric about $x = \mu$ with total area $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = 1$.
Definition 3.1 & Theorems 3.1–3.2: Standardization Slide 12
The normal distribution $N(0, 1)$ (mean $0$, variance $1$) is the standard normal distribution: $$X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2) \iff Z = \frac{X - \mu}{\sigma} \sim N(0, 1)$$ $$Z \sim N(0, 1) \iff \sigma Z + \mu \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2), \qquad aX + b \sim N(a\mu + b,\, a^2\sigma^2)$$
